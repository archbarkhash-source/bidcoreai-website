What it is

Quantity Takeoff, and Why the Units Decide Everything

Quantity takeoff is the measurement of what a project physically requires, expressed in the units it is bought and installed in. Concrete in cubic yards. Formwork in square feet of contact area. Rebar in tons. Drywall in sheets or square feet. Conduit in linear feet by size. Doors in each. The list is the skeleton of the estimate — every cost line hangs off a quantity, and a quantity in the wrong unit is worse than a missing one, because it prices out to a plausible number nobody questions.

That is why quantity takeoff software is not just a measuring tool. It has to know that a wall area needs its openings deducted but its corners counted once, that pipe is bought in lengths and priced per foot, that a 10% waste factor on flooring is normal and a 10% waste factor on structural steel is not. BidcoreAI carries units, deductions and factors per scope item, and shows the factor as applied rather than baking it into the number.

The measuring itself runs on the PDF plan set in the browser. AI proposes quantities for repeating conditions across every sheet; an estimator reviews them against the drawings and the specifications. Quantities can be grouped by scope, by level, by phase or by bid package, so the same takeoff produces a supplier material list, a subcontractor package and an estimate structure without being measured three times.

Everything stays traceable. Each quantity records the sheet, the scale and the person who confirmed it, and exports to Excel with those references intact. Whether the number ends up in a bid form, a purchase order or a schedule of values, someone can follow it back to a line on a drawing — which is the whole point of doing a takeoff instead of estimating by square-foot rule of thumb.

Units

Quantities in the Units the Trade Buys In

Measured once, reported in whichever unit the supplier, the subcontractor and the estimate each need.

Count Quantities (EA)

Anything discrete and repeating, counted across the whole set rather than one sheet at a time.

  • Doors, windows, fixtures, devices
  • Equipment, diffusers and sprinkler heads
  • Footings, columns and piers
  • Reconciled against the schedules
Linear Quantities (LF)

Runs traced on the drawing, split by size, type or level so they can be bought and priced correctly.

  • Walls, partitions and footings
  • Pipe and conduit by diameter
  • Duct by size and gauge
  • Trim, base, curb and edging
Area Quantities (SF / SY)

Areas with deductions handled explicitly, because gross and net are different numbers with different uses.

  • Floor, wall, ceiling and roof areas
  • Openings deducted or retained by rule
  • Paving in SF or SY
  • Formwork contact area
Volume Quantities (CY / CF)

Depth taken from the sections and details, never inferred from plan area alone.

  • Concrete by element and mix
  • Excavation, cut, fill and haul
  • Aggregate base and subbase
  • Topsoil strip and respread
Weight Quantities (LB / TON)

Weights calculated off schedules and section properties rather than assumed as a ratio of another quantity.

  • Rebar by bar size and length
  • Structural steel by section
  • Ductwork by gauge and area
  • Miscellaneous metals
Material Quantities

Measured quantities converted into what actually gets ordered — sheets, rolls, bags, sticks and pallets.

  • Board and sheet counts
  • Rolls, bundles and pallets
  • Bags, gallons and cartridges
  • Rounded to purchase units

Workflow

From Plan Set to Quantity Sheet

The sequence that keeps units, factors and references intact all the way to the export.

1
Load the drawings and schedules
Upload the plan set with its specification book and schedules. Schedules matter here: they are what a counted quantity gets reconciled against.
2
Calibrate each sheet
Scale is set from a known dimension and stored per sheet, so measurements taken later inherit it and a reviewer can confirm what it was.
3
Define the scope items
Set up what you are measuring and in which units — wall type, pipe size, finish schedule item — with the deduction and waste rules each one needs.
4
Measure with AI assistance
The platform proposes quantities for repeating conditions across the set; the estimator confirms, corrects and adds what the drawings imply but do not draw.
5
Reconcile against schedules
Counts are compared to the door, window, fixture and finish schedules. A count that disagrees with the schedule is either a measurement error or an RFI — both worth knowing before bid day.
6
Apply factors and convert
Waste, lap and conversion factors are applied per item and shown as applied, then quantities are rounded to purchase units where a material summary needs it.
7
Export or price
Export the quantity sheet to Excel with its sheet references, send the material summary to suppliers, or price the quantities in the same project.

Quantity Takeoff Software — Your Questions Answered

Quantity takeoff software measures how much of everything a project requires, in the units it is bought and installed in — each, linear feet, square feet, cubic yards, tons. It is the measurement layer beneath a cost estimate: every price line hangs off a quantity, so the quantities have to be both accurate and correctly united before pricing means anything.
Takeoff is measurement; estimating is pricing. A takeoff says the project needs 4,200 square feet of 5/8" type X board and 1,140 linear feet of 3-5/8" metal stud. An estimate says what those cost, once labor productivity, waste, equipment, overhead and markup are applied. BidcoreAI does both in one workspace, which is why there is no export step between them.
