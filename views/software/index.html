What this section is

One Platform, a Workspace for Every Scope

Every page in this section describes the same platform doing a different job. The takeoff engine, the cost database, the bid tools and the proposal writer do not change between trades. What changes is what gets measured and in which units — cubic yards and formwork contact area for concrete, pounds of metal by gauge for ductwork, unit counts at coursing for masonry, head counts by hazard classification for sprinkler, cut and fill between two surfaces for earthwork.

That is the argument for a single system rather than a shelf of trade tools. A general contractor pricing a whole building measures every division in one project, against one cost database, with one set of drawings and one revision history. A subcontractor pricing one trade uses the same workspace scoped to their own scope, and gets the same audit trail: every quantity linked to the sheet it came from, every assumption written down.

The same is true downstream of the estimate. Bidding — packages, invitations, coverage, levelling and proposals — runs in the same project as the estimate that generated it, so the number you submit is the number you priced. Federal work adds solicitation reading, FAR clause review, wage determinations and proposals written to the evaluation criteria, on top of the same estimating engine.

The pages below go into the detail of each area — what the software does, how it does it, where bids in that scope usually go wrong, and what comes out at the end. Start with the head-term pages if you want the general argument, or go straight to what you are bidding this week.

Workflow

How a Bid Runs in BidcoreAI

The same seven steps whether you are pricing one trade or twenty-eight divisions.

1
Upload the drawings and specifications
Plan set, specification book, addenda and bid forms. Large sets can arrive as a Drive, Dropbox or WeTransfer link instead of an upload.
2
The AI reads the documents
Every sheet, schedule and specification section is analysed and the scope returned as a list, each item citing the page it came from.
3
Quantities are measured
The takeoff workspace measures across the whole set. AI proposes the measurements; an estimator confirms, corrects and adds what only a person would catch.
4
Review the measurements
Every quantity is shown on its sheet at the scale it was measured, so a reviewer can verify a number without re-measuring it.
5
Apply your costs
Quantities priced against your own cost library or a regional benchmark, with productivity, waste and equipment carried into the line item.
6
Package and level
The estimate splits into subcontractor packages, ITBs go out with the right sheets, and returning quotes are levelled against the scope sent.
7
Export or submit
Export to Excel for your own bid form, or generate the proposal from the priced estimate and submit it with the assumptions attached.
Benefits

Why One Platform Beats Four

The gains come from the connections between the steps, not from any single step.

No Export Between Steps

Takeoff, estimate, package and proposal share one data model, so there is no re-keying and no chance of pricing a superseded takeoff.

Costs That Compound

Every estimate feeds one cost database. After a season of bidding, your unit costs reflect your crews on your projects rather than a national average.

Auditable Numbers

Every quantity keeps its sheet, scale and author. When a number is challenged after award, you can show it instead of defending it.

More Bids per Estimator

The binding constraint on most contractors is estimator hours. Cutting takeoff time is what turns three properly priced bids a week into eight.

Fewer Scope Misses

Reading the specification systematically catches the sections a hurried estimator skims — the omissions that survive into the contract.

Work That Outlives People

Estimates, measurements, assumptions and correspondence live in the project rather than on one estimator's desktop.
