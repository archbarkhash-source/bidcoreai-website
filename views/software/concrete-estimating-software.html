What it is

Why Concrete Is the Easiest Trade to Underbid

A concrete estimate has three quantities, and only one of them is obvious. Cubic yards of concrete are easy to picture and comparatively easy to measure. Reinforcement and formwork are neither, and between them they usually carry more cost than the concrete itself. That is why so many concrete bids are lost on paper long before anything is poured: the yardage was right and the formwork was carried as a percentage.

BidcoreAI measures all three from the structural drawings. Volumes come from the sections and details — footing sizes, slab thicknesses, wall dimensions, column schedules — not from plan area with an assumed depth. Reinforcement is calculated from the bar schedules by bar size and length, giving a real tonnage rather than a pounds-per-cubic-yard rule of thumb that varies by a factor of three between a slab on grade and a shear wall. Formwork is measured as square feet of contact area, element by element, with the reuse count carried as an explicit assumption rather than hidden inside a unit rate.

Placement is priced by method and crew, because a pour reached by pump costs differently to one reached by crane or buggy, and a mat pour and a wall lift do not share a productivity rate. Finishing is priced against what the specification actually calls for — a burnished floor with a tight flatness class is not a broom finish with a different word in front of it.

The result is an estimate that survives a scope conversation. When a general contractor asks why your number differs from the one next to it, you can show cubic yards by element, tonnage by bar size, contact area by form type and the reuse count you assumed — and find out in five minutes whether the gap is a scope difference or a pricing one.

Scope

What the Software Measures on a Concrete Package

Measured from the structural set: plans, sections, details, column and bar schedules.

Foundations

Everything below the slab, measured from the sections rather than the foundation plan alone.

  • Spread and continuous footings
  • Grade beams and pile caps
  • Foundation and stem walls
  • Excavation and backfill interface
Slabs

On grade and elevated, separated by thickness because thickness drives both volume and finishing crew.

  • Slab on grade by thickness
  • Elevated and topping slabs
  • Vapour barrier and sub-base
  • Metal deck interface quantities
Vertical Concrete

Walls, columns and beams by section and lift height, which is what sets the forming cost.

  • Walls by thickness and height
  • Columns by section and lift
  • Beams and shear walls
  • Stairs, ramps and landings
Reinforcement

Weighed from the bar schedules by size and length — never derived as a ratio of concrete volume.

  • Rebar tonnage by bar size
  • Bar schedules, laps and bend lists
  • Welded wire and fibre reinforcement
  • Dowels, chairs and accessories
Formwork

Square feet of contact area by element type, with the reuse count recorded as an assumption.

  • SFCA by element type
  • Reuse and stripping cycles
  • Shoring and reshoring
  • Edge forms, bulkheads and blockouts
Placement and Finishing

Priced by pour method and finish class, because both change the crew and the productivity rate.

  • Pump, crane or buggy placement
  • Finish type and flatness class
  • Curing, sealing and joint sawing
  • Embeds, anchor bolts and testing

Benefits

What Changes on a Concrete Bid

Mostly it changes what you can prove when the number is challenged.

Formwork Priced, Not Assumed

Contact area measured element by element replaces the percentage-of-pour habit — the single most common reason a concrete bid is 15% out in either direction.

Rebar You Can Buy From

Tonnage by bar size is what a fabricator quotes against. A pounds-per-yard rule of thumb is not, and it is wrong in both directions depending on the element.

Volumes That Match the Pour

Measuring depth from the sections catches thickened edges, drop panels and stepped footings that plan-area estimating averages into nothing.

Realistic Placement Labor

Crew-based rates by pour type mean a job full of small wall lifts is not priced as though it were one big slab.

Addenda Handled

When the structural set is re-issued, revision comparison shows the elements that changed rather than sending you back to the beginning.

Assumptions in Writing

Reuse counts, over-pour allowances and finish classes are stated in the basis of estimate, so a scope argument stays about scope.
